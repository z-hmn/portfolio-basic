Design Suggestions for Flowcode

An AI feature that helps novice programmers see what they could try next — without deciding it for them. The hard part was never generating good ideas. It was delivering them without taking over.

Year

01 — Context

A coding environment
for people who are stuck.

Premise

Flowcode explores how AI can help novices understand and iterate — deliberately not how to generate code for them.

Flowcode is a creative-coding environment built in the Design Tools Lab that pairs HTML, CSS, and JavaScript editors with a live animation preview, a generated flowchart of code structure, and a beginner-oriented explanation chat.

It is explicitly positioned against vibe coding. The goal is comprehension and authorship, not output. But that creates a specific failure mode: a novice finishes a working animation, looks at it, and has no idea what they could plausibly do next. They are not blocked by syntax. They are blocked by not knowing what is possible.

How can we support novice programmers in identifying opportunities to iterate on creative coding projects?

Research question
02 — The Tension

Help that doesn’t
take over.

From the literature

Proactive programming assistance is most useful when it is “non-disruptive, context-aware.” Both halves turned out to be design constraints, not adjectives.

A suggestion feature has an obvious trap. Make it too passive and nobody finds it. Make it too assertive and it quietly becomes the author of the project — the learner stops choosing and starts accepting.

That tension is the actual design problem, and it drove every decision. The feature had to be specific enough to be useful — grounded in the learner’s real code, naming their actual elements — while remaining easy to ignore. Suggestions describe a direction, never hand over a finished implementation.

The measure of success was correspondingly unusual. Not “did users accept the suggestions,” but “did users still feel like the decisions were theirs.”

03 — Process

From literature
to shipped feature.

Literature review

Surveyed research on novice programming education, proactive AI support, and creative coding to establish what “helpful” needed to mean here.

Design exploration

Low-fidelity sketches testing different placements, triggers, and levels of assertiveness for surfacing an idea to a learner.

Prototype

Refined the strongest concepts into Figma mockups and lightweight prototypes, narrowing a broad idea space to one interaction model.

Implement & evaluate

Built the feature in React with a custom prompt pipeline, then evaluated it in a three-hour workshop with K‑8 STEM teachers.

04 — Iteration

Three versions,
one problem.

Through-line

Each revision moved the same content further out of the learner’s way while keeping it equally reachable.

The three iterations are best read as successive answers to one question: where does a suggestion live so that it is available but not imposing?

Every revision reduced how much the feature asserted itself — and that is what made it usable.

Design principle
05 — The Feature

One idea, on request,
in your own project.

Deliberate constraints

One suggestion at a time. Named against the learner’s own SVG elements. Always regenerable. Never auto-applied.

Design Suggestions generates a single, project-specific, achievable idea — phrased in plain language and anchored to elements that actually exist in the learner’s code, like #back-fin or #cloud.

Showing one suggestion rather than a list was deliberate: a list invites comparison shopping and shifts the learner into evaluating the AI’s options instead of forming their own intent. If the single suggestion doesn’t appeal, regenerating is one click — a cheap dismissal, not a rejection of a menu.

The show me how action passes the suggestion into the explanation chat rather than writing code directly. Implementation stays the learner’s job; the feature only ever supplies direction.

06 — Evaluation

Eleven teachers,
three hours.

Setup

K‑8 STEM educators, most with little or no coding experience. All successfully built animations in Flowcode during the session.

The survey targeted the thing the design was actually optimizing for. Every single respondent agreed or strongly agreed that they remained in control of their own creative decisions — with no neutral responses at all, the strongest and least ambiguous result in the study.

Strongly favourable Favourable Neutral Unfavourable

Bars are coloured by outcome, not by Likert label, so the reverse-scored item below reads in the same direction as the rest.

I felt that I remained in control of the creative decisions I made while using the suggestion feature.

The suggestions felt relevant to the project and my current code.

The suggestion feature helped me think of ideas I wouldn’t have come up with on my own.

The suggestions felt repetitive. (reverse-scored — disagreement is the good outcome)

Post-workshop survey, N = 11 respondents per item. Percentages rounded.

Honest reading

Relevance and agency both landed. The weaker result is real and worth stating plainly: roughly a quarter of participants found the suggestions repetitive. With one suggestion surfaced at a time and no memory of what had already been shown, the generator could circle the same handful of ideas within a session.

There is also a finding that sits outside the feature itself. Many participants needed help phrasing prompts at all — which suggests LLM prompting is now a curricular topic in its own right, not a skill that can be assumed.

Adapt to experience level

Proactive suggestions should scale their specificity to how much the learner already knows.

Track what was shown

Suggestion history would directly address the repetition finding — the clearest next iteration.

Connect the two features

Linking suggestions to code-to-preview highlighting would let learners see exactly which element a suggestion refers to.

07 — Takeaway

Interfaces decide
what feels possible.

Connection

The same concern as the waste-routing work: a representation determines what its user can see, and therefore what they can choose.

What a tool surfaces defines the space a person believes they are working in. A suggestion feature is not a neutral convenience — it shapes what a learner imagines is achievable next.

That is why the evaluation centred on retained agency rather than acceptance rate. A system that produced excellent suggestions while hollowing out the learner’s sense of authorship would have failed at the thing it was built to do, no matter how good the output looked.

I’m drawn to systems that support interpretation and exploration rather than automating decisions on someone’s behalf — and this project is the clearest test I’ve run of what that costs to build.

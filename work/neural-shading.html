Neural Shading for Indoor Architectural Scenes

Can a network infer how a room is lit from geometry and material alone? I trained a U‑Net to find out. It learned the soft structure of illumination and never learned a shadow — and the reason why is the actual finding.

01 — Premise

Learning light
instead of simulating it.

Approach

Not rendering. Illumination framed as a supervised image‑to‑image problem: geometry and material in, light out.

Physically-based rendering produces realistic light by simulating how it actually travels — bouncing, occluding, accumulating across an entire scene. It is accurate and it is slow.

Neural shading asks whether part of that can be learned instead. Following Nalbach et al.’s Deep Shading, the idea is to map per-pixel scene attributes directly to appearance, skipping the simulation. I narrowed it to one supervised subproblem: predict diffuse illumination from surface normals, depth, and diffuse reflectance.

The appeal for architectural work is obvious — fast, plausible interior lighting without a render farm. The interesting part turned out to be where that shortcut breaks.

Can a neural network infer diffuse illumination from local geometric and material information alone?

Research question
02 — Data

A dataset that ships
its own ground truth.

Why Hypersim

It factors each rendered image into reflectance, illumination, and a residual. That decomposition is what makes supervision possible at all.

Apple’s Hypersim provides photorealistic synthetic indoor scenes with dense per-pixel buffers already separated out. Because the renderer knows what illumination was, illumination can be a training target rather than something inferred.

I used four buffers: normal_world and normal_cam for orientation, depth_meters for layout, diffuse_reflectance for material, and diffuse_illumination as ground truth. Roughly 2,000 samples across 10 scenes, split 80/20.

Preprocessing mattered more than expected. Illumination is high dynamic range, so values were tone mapped with x / (1 + x); depth was log-scaled and normalized; normals clipped to [−1, 1] and reflectance to [0, 1]. Everything resized to 256 × 256 and concatenated into a seven-channel tensor.

Engineering note

The first bottleneck was not the GPU. It was the loader.

Early training was painfully slow because every epoch re-opened HDF5 files and re-ran preprocessing per sample. I built a caching layer that does that work once and writes serialized PyTorch tensors to disk. GPU utilization went from intermittent to saturated, which is what made the controlled experiments below affordable in the first place.

03 — Model

Seven channels in,
three channels out.

Architecture

A U‑Net: encoder compresses, decoder rebuilds, skip connections carry high-frequency detail across the bottleneck.

The encoder steps 7 → 32 → 64 → 128 channels while halving resolution to a 256-channel bottleneck at 32 × 32. The decoder upsamples back to full resolution, concatenating skip features at each stage, and a final convolution projects to three channels of RGB illumination.

The prediction is then recombined with reflectance — reflectance × illumination — to reconstruct diffuse appearance. That recombination is not just for display; it becomes a loss term, and it turned out to be the most important one.

04 — Experiments

Five controlled changes,
one stubborn result.

Held constant

Same ~2,000 samples, same U‑Net, same training setup. Only one variable moves at a time.

The baseline model — plain L1 between predicted and ground-truth illumination — converged numerically and produced blurry, averaged light. Every experiment after that was an attempt to find out why, by changing one thing and watching what moved.

Camera vs. world normals

World-space normals gave slightly better spatial coherence — lighting depends on how a surface is oriented in the room, not relative to the camera. A small but physically sensible gain.

Reconstruction loss

Added L1 on reflectance × illumination, not just illumination. The single clearest improvement of the project: optimizing for the rendered consequence rather than the intermediate tensor.

Gradient loss

Compared horizontal and vertical illumination gradients to push for sharper transitions. Produced more spatial variation. Still no hard shadow boundaries.

Model capacity

Doubled feature widths from base‑32 to base‑64. Optimization improved marginally; visual quality did not. Strong evidence that size was not the bottleneck.

Perceptual loss

Compared reconstructions in VGG16 feature space instead of pixel space, to escape the blur that pixel losses reward. Slight brightness shifts, no recovery of edges or shadows.

What the curves show

Train and test fall together, and keep falling. The model is not overfitting — and it is not improving where it matters.

Base‑32 total loss drops from 0.142 to 0.070 on train and 0.118 to 0.068 on test over twenty epochs. Base‑64 reaches a slightly lower floor in fewer epochs. In both, the two curves stay locked together — healthy, well-behaved optimization.

And yet the images barely change. That gap is the most useful thing the experiments produced: the loss and the goal had come apart. L1 error keeps rewarding a smoother average, which is exactly the wrong answer.

05 — Result

It learned the room.
It never learned the shadow.

Read the two left panels

Ground-truth illumination has a crisp pool of light on the wall. The prediction has a soft grey suggestion of one.

The best model — world-space normals, reconstruction and gradient loss — reliably recovers the broad brightness structure of a scene. It knows which wall is brighter, roughly where light falls off, and how the space is laid out. Multiplied back through reflectance, the reconstruction is genuinely close to the target.

What it never recovers is anything high-frequency: sharp shadows, corner darkening, occlusion boundaries, contact regions. The error map is not noisy everywhere — it concentrates precisely on edges and object contacts. The failure has a shape, and the shape is informative.

The loss kept improving. The shadows never arrived.

Finding
06 — Why It Failed

The problem was
underdetermined.

The diagnosis

Illumination is a global phenomenon. The model only ever sees a single camera’s screen-space slice of the room.

A shadow exists because something the viewer may not be able to see is blocking a light source the viewer may also not be able to see. Predicting it requires knowing where the lights are, what the full geometry is, and what occludes what.

The model receives none of that. It gets normals, depth, and reflectance for visible pixels from one viewpoint — no light positions, no complete 3D geometry, no visibility relationships, no multi-view context. Two rooms with identical visible geometry and materials but different light placement produce identical inputs and different correct answers.

Given contradictory targets for identical inputs, the optimum under an L1 objective is to predict the average. The blur is not a bug in the training. It is the correct answer to an underdetermined question — which is why no loss function and no amount of capacity fixed it.

Limitations
  • Screen space only. One camera, visible surfaces only. No occluded geometry and no scene-level context enter the model at any point.
  • No light supervision. Light source position, intensity, and visibility are never provided, explicitly or implicitly.
  • Dataset scale. ~2,000 samples is small for image synthesis. Scale is unlikely to be the core issue, but it has not been ruled out.
  • Diffuse only. Specular and non-Lambertian effects are outside the problem as framed.

Supervise the consequence

Reconstruction loss beat everything else because it optimized for the rendered result rather than the intermediate tensor the model happened to output.

Capacity was never the limit

Doubling width moved the loss and not the images. When more parameters change nothing visible, the bottleneck is upstream of the model.

Inputs bound outputs

No objective can recover information the input representation never contained. Loss design cannot substitute for missing context.

07 — Takeaway

A model can only see
what you give it.

Connection

The same concern as the waste-routing and Flowcode work: what a representation includes determines what can be concluded from it.

The useful outcome here was not a working shading network. It was a precise account of why this framing cannot produce one — and the difference between “the model underperformed” and “the input representation makes the task impossible” is the entire value of the project.

Getting to that claim required ruling things out honestly: better losses, better normals, more capacity, perceptual supervision. Each experiment removed a candidate explanation until only the representation was left. That is a slower answer than a good demo, and a far more transferable one.

It also points somewhere specific. The directions that would actually help — attention or transformer-based global context, explicit light and visibility modeling, multi-scale or volumetric scene representations — are all ways of giving the model more of the scene, not ways of making the model bigger.

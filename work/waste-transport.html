Modeling Residential Waste Transportation in New York City

The city tells you how much waste each district produces. It does not tell you how that waste moves. This project builds three competing answers — and treats the difference between them as the finding.

01 — Premise

A system you can weigh
but cannot see.

The gap

Public data gives tonnage, districts, and facility locations — but no route-level geography. The movement itself is missing.

Sanitation is among the most visible infrastructural systems in New York City, and among the least legible. You can find out how many tons a district produced last month. You cannot find out how those tons travelled.

That opacity has consequences. When waste is represented only as an aggregate quantity per district, the spatial cost of moving it disappears. Route intensity, neighborhood exposure to waste corridors, and the relationship between transport patterns and demographic conditions all become impossible to examine.

So rather than attempt to reconstruct the Department of Sanitation’s actual operational routes — which are not public — this project models plausible routing logics and compares them. The question is not which routes are real. It is what different assumptions about routing produce when you follow them all the way through to a map.

How do different routing assumptions shape the spatial distribution of modeled waste transportation — and what do those patterns have to do with who lives there?

Research question
02 — Data Preparation

Getting from district
tonnage to section origins.

Proxy variable

Residential units from MapPLUTO stand in for waste generation. This is an assumption, and it is the first one the model makes.

DSNY reports refuse tonnage at the district scale, but districts are too coarse to model collection origins. District sections are the finer operational unit — so district tonnage has to be pushed down to them.

I aggregated residential unit counts from MapPLUTO for both districts and sections, then distributed each district’s annual tonnage across its sections in proportion to their share of residential units. Section centroids then became the modeled route origins.

03 — Network Construction

Building the space
of possible routes.

Note

Candidate routes are not final routes. They define which facility connections each methodology is allowed to choose from.

I built a truck-eligible road network from NYC LION street centerlines in ArcGIS Pro, then ran a closest-facility analysis from every section centroid to the five nearest disposal or transfer facilities.

That output is the routing universe — the constrained set of possibilities from which all three methodologies subsequently draw. Fixing this universe first is what makes the later comparison fair: the three models differ only in how they choose, never in what they are choosing from.

04 — Three Routing Models

Same city, same network,
three assumptions.

Route intensity

Routes buffered 25 ft, clipped to census tracts, then tonnage-weighted by length within each tract. This becomes the unit of all later analysis.

Each model assigns section tonnage to facilities under a different rule. Method 1 optimizes only for proximity. Method 2 introduces facility capacity through a Python optimization routine. Method 3 abandons single-facility assignment entirely and splits each section’s tonnage across two facilities, weighted by distance and capacity.

05 — Spatial Clustering

Where intensity
concentrates.

Method

Local Moran’s I on tract-level route intensity, identifying statistically significant clusters and spatial outliers.

Across all three models the same places keep surfacing: the South Bronx, Upper Manhattan, Staten Island, and parts of North Brooklyn. These are not artifacts of one routing rule. They are what the fixed geography of facilities and the structure of the truck-eligible network produce no matter which logic you apply.

The differences are just as informative. Method 1 fragments intensity radially around many destinations. Method 2 consolidates it near larger facilities. Method 3 — despite distributing waste across more facilities — generates the strongest clustering of all.

Increasing routing flexibility did not diffuse concentration. It intensified shared corridor dependence.

Finding — Method 3
06 — Demographic Association

What the corridors
run through.

Method

Lee’s L measures bivariate spatial association — not simple correlation — between route intensity and demographic surfaces.

One result here has to be read carefully. Total population correlates with route intensity partly by construction, because residential units were the proxy used to allocate tonnage in the first place. Reporting that as a finding would be circular, so I don’t.

The Supplemental Security Income surface is the interpretively useful one. Across all three methods, modeled route intensity visibly co-occurs with areas of socioeconomic vulnerability — particularly in the South Bronx, North Brooklyn, and Southeast Queens.

This identifies a spatial coincidence. It does not demonstrate exposure, health burden, or causation, and I want to be precise about that distinction rather than let the map overclaim.

07 — What the Model Cannot Say

Stating the assumptions
out loud.

Position

When the operational system is hidden, the model is a structured hypothesis — not a transparent representation of reality.

Limitations
  • Proxy allocation. Section tonnage is estimated from residential units, assuming relative uniformity of waste generation within districts. MAUP uncertainty is carried through everything downstream.
  • Centroids are not pickup points. They are spatial proxies that compress the distributed reality of curbside collection into a single origin per section.
  • Static shortest paths. The models ignore truck capacity, traffic, time-of-day routing, sanitation schedules, marine transfer timing, garage locations, and collection sequencing.
  • First-degree destinations only. Many facilities modeled here are transfer stations, not final disposal. The regional geography of waste beyond city limits is out of scope.
08 — Takeaway

The method is
the finding.

Assumptions become geographies

Three defensible routing rules over identical data produced materially different maps of where the city’s waste burden falls.

Some structure is not optional

Persistent corridors across all methods show where facility siting and network topology constrain outcomes regardless of routing logic.

Opacity is methodologically consequential

When operational systems are not public, modeling choices quietly become analytical claims. Naming them is part of the work.

Why it matters

This is a replicable approach for interrogating infrastructure that is partially inaccessible: build several defensible models, compare them honestly, and treat the divergence between them as evidence about the method rather than noise to be resolved.

The geographies that become visible are inseparable from the assumptions used to construct them. That is the argument the project is actually making — waste transportation is the case it makes the argument through.

Modeling Residential Waste Transportation in New York City

NYC publishes how much residential waste each sanitation district collects, but not the routes that waste takes to transfer and disposal facilities. This project developed three routing models using the same road network and compared how different assignment methods affected the spatial distribution of waste transport.

01

Data and routing model

I aggregated MapPLUTO residential units by DSNY district and section, then allocated each district’s annual refuse tonnage to sections in proportion to their residential units. Section centroids served as route origins. This assumes similar waste generation per residential unit within each district; changing the allocation or spatial boundaries would change the estimates.

I built a truck-eligible network from NYC LION street centerlines in ArcGIS Pro. Network Analyst generated routes from each section centroid to its five nearest disposal or transfer facilities. All three methods use this network and candidate-route set.

02

Three routing methods

To compare route intensity by census tract, I buffered routes by 25 feet, clipped them to tract boundaries, and weighted them by tonnage and route length within each tract.

03

Spatial analysis and results

High-intensity concentrations persist in the South Bronx, Upper Manhattan, Staten Island, and North Brooklyn. Minimum-distance routing spreads intensity around nearby facilities; capacity adjustment consolidates it near larger facilities. The two-facility model produces the strongest clustering, with overlapping routes concentrating tonnage along shared corridors.

The recurring areas suggest that facility locations and network connections influence the patterns across assignment rules. Staten Island’s limited connections and single in-city facility constrain its modeled routes.

Lee’s L identifies spatial association between modeled route intensity and SSI percentage, an indicator of socioeconomic vulnerability, particularly in the South Bronx, North Brooklyn, and Southeast Queens. These are modeled geographic associations, not measurements of truck exposure, pollution, health burden, or causation.

Population associations require additional caution: allocating tonnage by residential units already links the model to where people live. Treating that relationship as an independent demographic finding would risk circular reasoning.

Limitations

  • Routes are modeled, not observed DSNY truck movements.
  • Residential-unit allocation and section centroids simplify waste generation and pickup locations; aggregation introduces uncertainty, including the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem.
  • Static shortest paths omit truck capacity, traffic, collection schedules and sequencing, garage locations, and marine transfer timing.
  • The model ends at the first facility. Transfers and disposal beyond those destinations, including outside NYC, are out of scope.
